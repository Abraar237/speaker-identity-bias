Answer bank

24 items, one medium quality tier (target score band 40–75 — the script-bias predecessor found only its medium tier had headroom to move; the high tier saturated near 99, the low tier floored near 15). L2-ARCTIC (24 non-native English speakers — Hindi, Arabic, Mandarin, Korean, Spanish, Vietnamese L1s) against matched native CMU ARCTIC recordings, reading 8 shared prompt sentences confirmed present across every speaker.

240 real clips, $0 generation cost. CP1 verified license (CC BY-NC 4.0, registration-gated) and sentence overlap — but coverage isn't uniform per speaker (974–1,132 of 1,132 sentences), so the 8 shared IDs are confirmed present for every speaker before selection. 08

Analysis

One scripted pass, no hand-computed numbers.

Unit of analysis: within-item score difference against a reference condition (US voice for the TTS arm, native CMU ARCTIC for the real-speech anchor), per judge and condition. Bootstrap 95% CIs (10,000 resamples), two-sided sign-flip permutation tests (20,000 permutations), paired effect size dz. If a pre-registered direction turns out backwards, that reversal is reported exactly as plainly as a confirmation — this is what pre-registration is for.

Budget / power trade-off

The $20 hard stop is tighter than the predecessor script-bias paper's design, which ran n=150 items. This plan uses n=24 for the TTS arm — confidence intervals on the accent-shift estimate will be wider, and a true effect near the smaller end of that paper's range (+1 to +1.4 points in some conditions) may not clear significance at this N.

The real-speech anchor (240 clips, $0 generation cost) partly offsets this — it's the cheapest place to buy back power. If Axis A results come back inconclusive at CP3, the recommended fix is enlarging the real-speech anchor's sentence count before spending more on synthetic TTS items.
